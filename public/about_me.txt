Nearing the end of Toronto's first lockdown a poem titled “Here I am” was anonymously taped up to the broad, showcase window of my shuttered local. It was a eulogy doing no one any good or justice I was afraid, that much I knew without reading past the title, glancing at it on my walks nowhere, around the neighbourhood. Noel’s death was more or less old news by then. A regular had told me the whole story on a day crisp with the promise of spring, acknowledging me right there on the street as never before.
She carried on like she’d witnessed the whole thing, but he'd died alone—that much was clear. When it occurred to me, finally, that it wasn't the virus that had done him in, that he hadn't even been sick but simply fell by accident, freakishly cracking the back of his skull on a granite towelholder in his very own bathroom, I got woozy. She cut her musing, about how many days late the paramedics must have arrived, short and certainly grabbed my shoulders, holding me steady. Dude: she hollered, not wearing a mask. I couldn’t remember her name either and focused on its blank ambience as I regained my composure, hands on knees with Noel’s face passing unseen through the dark.
I stopped in front of it one night to drunkenly peer inside, then stood there scanning the verse numbers like a stray trying to make sense of its own missing poster, in the lamp light. It had been Noel's place Crossroads, a barbershop primarily but beyond the half-dozen big chairs plainly visible from the street there was a small, wooden bar running toward the back, with just enough room to squeeze behind the stools, across which he served booze on Sunday nights. It was a beacon for misfits from all over our end of the city, with everything else on the strip and into the suburbs it tailed through shutdown as the weekend dissipated under the dreary anticipation of a new start.
As a rule of thumb I stayed clear of the nightlife in my neighborhood; all I knew about why I had moved to this city was that I didn't want to feel like the trapped, nervous centre of a small town's concrete lack of imagination anymore. And yet I found myself at Crossroads habitually if not regularly, going on a decade. The impossible intimacy of the place was like a scary ride, and Noel played the music softly, all the better to hear himself bantering.
I liked the corner stool at the far end, and sat there saying nothing with my fernet, eavesdropping on the show. Noel was larger than life, a clown good at drawing people out of themselves; he had a witty, special touch of sympathy for each of his patrons and called everyone friend. It was love of the stranger by a simple change of the label, collapsing the distance between us without fear seemingly.
I'd finally relax, letting him do all the work and become happily withdrawn there, lapsing into envy occasionally when a woman would gaze at him, gazelessly. He was so unfailingly solicitous with me despite my remove, checking on my drink more often than I cared for. Here’s to the savviest guy I know he’d say, clicking my glass like I’d never live down my past.
Crossroads was where I'd go to read myself slowly, as vaguely reflected in the caricatures I made of all those crowded, illegible characters. It’s where I abandoned the endless longing of the poetic for the poetry of returning home; where I’d backstep through the ground zero of being forgotten and fall upon the community of being a lone.
The irony! It’s an ego rebirth simulator, I laughed remembering him in the empty street, a summary of the nebulous path it took to like love myself as a neighbour: my point of entry into this city, its next draft shining all about me.
